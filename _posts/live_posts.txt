I wasted a good few weeks on the same problem everyone muses on at least once in their life: "Can I make a lot of money in a hurry, by doing very little?"

No, I couldn't.

Ironically, I'd been reading *Fortune's Formula* by William Poundstone, in which the work of Claude Shannon is discussed. Shannon *did* make an obscene amount of money as an investor, but he had insight into industries that were going to succeed, and his strategy was the typical "buy and forget for a few decades" that every get-rich-quick bemoans. "Shannon's Demon" was his hypothetical rapid-trading money maker, assuming a world of no fees; if only, Dr. Shannon. Why I therefore believed I could beat the man who founded information theory, and is in part the namesake of the Claude chatbot itself, is beyond me retrospectively. Moreso, I don't think it actually occurred to me that a great many smarter people came before me, I just wanted to see what would happen.

The advantage of a statistician's background is a double edged sword, as for every seemingly viable strategy I developed, I could develop an equally good reason why picking randomly, or picking an ETF, was as good or better. Like Shannon, I also found methods that would be genuinely viable in a world with no exchange fees; I suppose I'd also be an excellent doctor in a world without disease.

Besides the fact I never developed a genuinely viable strategy, you do find some interesting behaviours in markets. Usually, a strategy based on choosing assets that maximise return per unit volatility while minimising correlation between equities was quite strong, as was diversifying across markets as well. However, we should always do our research. I nearly fell into a pothole when one model suggested a number of NZX listed small cap companies. Blindly, I placed orders. Rapidly, I cancelled orders. Firstly, I later disproved this strategy had an edge, and found that it may as well have sent me backwards. Second, I found one of the companies had flatlined in its valuation for two months. This was a business available to be traded regularly, that no one was buying, selling, or even talking about. To date, it remains the single most stagnant asset I have ever seen; definitely not worth my money.

However, what I *did* find of value is actually somewhat humorous, and for that we need a diatribe. Part of the motivation of this strategy is simple: I'm broke. A student in NZ relies on student loans and tutoring work during the year, and internships or family during the holidays. Until recently, I fell in the glorious category of being responsible for rent, utilities, and feeding myself without a job - though my family have graciously supported me with food occasionally. I think most students stare down a financial abyss at some point, and make some *fascinating* decisions. For me, I went vegan. It sucked. I have had good vegan food, but it takes a particular talent I lack. However, it was definitely saving me money. It was during this time I was trying to work out the most filling things to eat, and was musing on some advice some horticulturalists had given me regarding flour prices that made me wonder a new question - how do commodities work?

Sharesies and similar platforms have been instrumental in improving financial education in NZ, as there is a stronger incentive to learn when there is the opportunity to invest. For this reason, I also strongly advocate for the democratisation of debt markets and similar, but forget that for now. The main thing here is that in NZ, for years the focus was property, then it was shifting towards equities, now it's getting a job (times are tough). However, one asset class that just sort of floats in and out of public perception is commodities. Namely gold. Commodities are a very popular modelling problem in time series, and in particular when we looked at gold and silver earlier this year in a professional skills for statisticians course (a good albeit dry paper) we saw how "weird" these assets have been in recent memory. So, I started playing with some models, particularly on wheat.

Commodities are a deceptively broad class of asset. Typically we think of precious metals such as silver and gold, or energy products like oil and natural gas. But really, wheat, soybeans, coffee, milk, beef, platinum, iridium, palladium, copper, aluminium, corn, wood, and practically any other resource is traded as a commodity. Even here, the complexity of contracts on what price each person buys and sells for still in some cases sounds like gambling with extra steps - more so than investing already does to be fair. A 7% margin of error was something I was quite pleased to achieve on wheat prices, but the extra information we contain beyond a financial time series makes the forecast naive. How can it know about El Niño? Or the situation in the Middle East?

What I learned from the horticulturalists was that this summer will be expensive. Grain has not left the Black Sea due to the Russo-Ukrainian war, while fertiliser has not left the Strait of Hormuz, and after a very dry previous summer and lack of fertiliser, Australia has not planted much either, while the US will in all likelihood, hoard. This results in international wheat prices increasing, and all wheat products too - all flour products. This will have a knock on effect to human health, not only due to food consumption, but also in micronutrients. NZ soil is selenium poor, we often use Australian wheat to make up for it. We also add folate to bread, and iodised salt. But if bread is unaffordable, people will not get these nutrients. Equally, if we continue to purchase bread anyway, then general living standards will decrease.

The Malthusian model is a horrifying concept, claiming that the only way to meaningfully improve living standards is if a sizeable portion of the population dies off, but I worry that we may yet live to see such a reality play out as it did with the Black Plague in the 1300s. Only here, the disease is not of pestilence, but our own making.

So I sat back, pondering how I arrived at global health concerns and a pariah economic model when I just wanted to get rich, and realised how extremely lucky I am. The classic "grass is always greener on the other side" seems to ignore the less fortunate sides, indeed even when we may have just crawled out of these ourselves.

My core takeaway? Just take the ETFs, and diversify a bit into fixed income and commodities while you're at it.

Genetics is fascinating, that's why I'm more than happy to leave it to the geneticists.

I'd been attending some workshops in statistical genetics recently, more out of curiosity than anything. Well, that and I was bored.

However, an interesting concept that makes total sense when you think about it was the relationship between heterozygosity and inbreeding. Without excessive detail, everyone inherits two alleles (versions) of the same gene from each parent. If these alleles are different, then you are heterozygous. If we now consider a population where most people are homozygous (inherited the same version of allele from each parent), then we generally know that either that allele is the most valuable for that environment, or that there has been some level of inbreeding, due to related individuals with the same alleles breeding and passing them on to their children.

I imagine some ethics committee is getting ready to cane me for talking about this hypothetical surrounding humans, so let's pivot to aardvarks. If we have three separate populations of aardvark, perhaps separated by a geographical barrier such as a deep river or a mountain range, then it is unlikely an aardvark will travel to another population, even if they both swiped right. This means that within each population, the total gene pool is fixed, with only mutation slowly bringing in new alleles. If instead the distance was maybe a few kilometers walk, then our aardvark might trundle off for a lovely evening and a few years of unplanned parenthood. In doing so, there have been alleles from population A introduced to population B, thus increasing the genetic diversity of population B - great! Tough luck for A though, they just lost a family member, and their gene pool is no better off if not worse.

Aardvarks are not an endangered species, but Kiwi are, so let's change tack and focus on the Rowi, the rarest Kiwi of all.

When a population is small, then the gene pool is also very small, and there comes great risk to the entire species of extinction, particularly by disease. The Rowi population once fell below 200 birds, a very small gene pool. Kiwi also have the unendearing trait of being able to cross-breed, but not necessarily produce fertile offspring. So, what could we do?

People who have built entire careers on such questions and birds have very good answers here, rooted in conservation practices, genetics, ecology, and animal husbandry. I propose a hyper un-realistic solution spanning hundreds of years on islands that do not exist with a larger population of birds than we actually have. I propose this not explicitly for Rowi, but to illustrate a point I will return to later. First, what is this cockamamie idea?

Firstly, assume we can drop a decent-sized population of birds onto a set of $n$ predator-free offshore islands. Since these birds cannot fly, they cannot migrate between islands. Let's also suppose these islands all have different selection pressures: climates, geographies, native predators, food abundance, disease, etc. We now leave these birds alone for centuries. This is a stochastic process, or really a set of them. Each island has a population that fluctuates with time, including the non-zero risk of extinction. Because these islands each differ, birds will evolve on them differently, with mutations introducing new alleles over successive generations. On each island, sub-populations may emerge. Say some birds wander across a valley and get stuck, resulting in a population only on the north-facing side of the island with warmer temperatures and more berries than grubs, resulting in birds with less plumage and are more herbivorous than insectivorous. 

At this point, we have a set of $n$ island populations, where each island quite likely has a sub-population or so depending on geographical barriers and factors about the island such as size (larger islands may develop more sub-populations). Assuming each island has a population close to carrying capacity, due to having been left alone for centuries, we now take a sample of birds from each island at random, and introduce all of these birds into another new environment, such as mainland NZ. This process has essentially "forced" $n$ distinct island populations, each with an unknown number of sub-populations, to mutate new traits and adapt to their environment. By bringing them together, we have achieved a genetic explosion. By introducing different birds to a new environment we now have a diversity of alleles that introduces greater resilience to the species, increasing their survival chances on the mainland (assuming we have improved the living environment somewhat). However, over time, each island will slowly increase in the average "kinship" or allele sharing between birds. For this reason, we now also introduce a small set of birds from each island to the other islands, introducing new alleles to each gene pool while also maintaining the population size on each island. 

In a few generations, the birds on the mainland will be totally different from each of the island populations, despite being descendants. By maintaining the island populations and regularly relocating birds between populations, including occasionally to the mainland, or even taking mainland birds back to the islands, we effectively produce subspecies or varieties that ensure genetic diversity.

However, does this not beg a sort of "Ship of Theseus" question: if we force these birds to evolve as separate populations and then bring them back together, did we really save the original species, or have we forced the evolution of multiple subspecies and derived what should theoretically be the closest analogue to the mainland species? I leave that for philosophers and conservationists, as this would never be in practice anyway.

That was some grunty explanation, but let me explain my point.

Fishing reserves.

There has been a lot of discourse in NZ about allowing fishing in reserves. I want to make it clear that I say this from a non-partisan point of view, but we should *never* allow fishing in these reserves. A common case study in statistics shows that the Goat Island reserve in North Auckland increased the fisheries population in the surrounding waters that *can* be fished more than if we *also* allow fishing within the reserve. A similar story is unfolding in Hahei, with fishery stocks increasing steadily. The value of these fisheries is they provide sanctuaries for the young fish to grow, but that they also provide grounds for population health. By ensuring waters where species can come together, we produce spaces where members of a species can breed and spawn, securing the population long term, while also ensuring that a new disease doesn't wipe out our entire fishing industry.

One reason NZ is a world leader in kiwifruit was our work in developing golden kiwifruit resistant to the Psa bacterium. Without genetic editing/engineering, this is only possible by having a sufficiently diverse population where we can identify alleles that when expressed make these plants resilient to disease. Even if we weren't dealing with the effects of climate change, pollution, and a host of factors changing the environment, the world rarely stays still - we need genetic diversity for both survival and plenty.
