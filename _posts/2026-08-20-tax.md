I may regret expressing these views - I mean them from an economic perspective, not a political one. The two are often difficult to separate.

Suffice to say I am pro-tax.

Anyone who has taken an introductory economics class will know that tax is used primarily to generate revenue for a government, but that effective tax can be used to incentivise certain behaviours we want to encourage. One end of this is the FIF tax here in NZ, which taxes unrealised gains on shares held overseas (with some exceptions) that were purchased for more than $50,000 NZD. This is a policy that incentivises those with large amounts of money they want to invest to keep it inside the country and *should* therefore foster growth in businesses within the country. The incentive is to *not* pay the tax, as it is extremely undesirable. The other end of this spectrum is the Trump administration tariffs. These were introduced to encourage US citizens to purchase more domestic products by increasing the cost of foreign products to compete with domestic ones - not accounting for the fact American labour is far more expensive than Vietnamese, for example. This provides leverage as it "encourages" countries to bend to American pressure and agree to certain deals, and it *should* support American business, and in theory helps with a trade deficit. 

These are good ideas in theory, but reality is messy, and rarely works as intended. If we consider FIF again, part of the issue here is that the NZ stock market is rather stagnant, and has been for a long period of time. This means that when penalised with investing in foreign assets, the domestic alternative is so unflattering that when paired with generous rules about property investment you end up with a country where the overwhelming majority of wealth is tied to property ownership. What's sad about this is we then hear so much doom and gloom about how property ownership is unattainable for younger generations or those on low incomes and how evil property owners are - when they aren't!

Fundamentally, can you really blame someone for making the decisions that best help them? Economic theory dictates that we should make the decisions that are in our best self-interest, so it's hardly surprising. Yes, there are some cases of total greed where people own in excess of 10 properties or treat tenants poorly, but for the average Kiwi this is not the case. This is where the tax argument becomes so problematic. Because of the vocal outcry on property prices, many parties are considering some form of capital gains or land tax, which I do support. It's not because I aspire to property ownership myself, in fact, I don't. I am extremely grateful to have lived in my parents' mortgage-free home, but when I see the extra hurdles they had to jump with rates, council regulations, and the hoops to jump through to renovate anything, I don't see the attraction in an incredibly expensive, non-liquid asset, especially when I prefer my lightweight, flexible lifestyle.

So why do I support a property tax of some form? Because property is not a productive investment. Owning a property contributes much less to an economy than investing in a business, as the property will house a family sure, but the business will grow, hiring more people, and contribute to the economy through production and taxation. This is not to mention that not all property even houses families either; landbanking has become such a disgusting problem that even in cities with unaffordable housing large swathes of land sit totally vacant as someone awaits a greater profit. These are rarely if ever the middle-class property owners too, as they rely on a mortgage with repayments so they need renters. The landbankers are so wealthy they hardly need such income.

This is our problem. The greed that comes with investing in financially viable yet non-productive assets. However, I don't entirely agree with FIF either. There is little wrong with Kiwis owning foreign assets, as this still contributes to the wealth of the nation through tax on dividends, but we do need to incentivise investment within our country as well. This is where we need tax to be used *effectively* to incentivise investment. Allow foreign ownership by all means without a cap, maintain the PIE tax on dividends, but tax all sales of foreign assets at 5% or some other low rate. I do not mean the profit, I mean the value it is sold back for. This incentivises long-term holding of assets, and disincentivises investment in higher-risk foreign assets more likely to result in a loss of money. Meanwhile, NZ shares remain tax free on capital gain (or maybe 5% tax on the profit only), and dividends are taxed at the PIE. This means the cost of investing in NZ is lower than investing elsewhere, but that elsewhere investment is still contributing to the NZ economy. I believe in this same way, we need better access to purchasing debt-instruments domestically, and that we would benefit from a government fund used to support new businesses which any citizen or permanent resident could buy into, and their money would be spread across businesses like an ETF. This same fund could also be used to finance government infrastructure projects if there is no demand for investment, and these would have to be issued as government bonds at a more competitive rate than the standard government bond. If there is still somehow a surplus of investment, this highly regulated and government controlled pool of funds would be allowed to invest in foreign assets *tax free*. This means that there is a strong incentive to invest in this fund from all ends of the spectrum, as it invests in young Kiwi businesses with growth potential, helps fund government projects, and has the potential to be your only access to international assets tax free.

I see a common argument against tax in terms of "the government doesn't use my tax properly!" to which I say "True". Objectively, there isn't a "right" way to use tax. I admit to wanting strong social services - free healthcare and education, benefits for those in need, providing job security to civil servants, and actually paying first responders properly. I do not find these to be outrageous desires myself, but I am biased. Each government knows they must do *some* form of balancing act across the wealth divide, and each party leans more towards one than the other. I'd love a world where the NZ government had to answer to a congress instead of being able to run around unchecked simply because they hold a majority, but I can see issues with that too. I think better would be to have ministries deliver forecasted outcomes of each policy and have these rejected if the perceived net benefit is negative. I see issues here too, but I strongly believe in the need for data-driven decision making and evidence-based policy creation. Shocking conclusions from a statistician, I know.

In an election year, I find it simply ridiculous to see how the parties decide to slander each other. I know my bias, I know where my vote will go, but that is not to say I am exactly impressed with "my side" either. One silver lining is flying too far below the radar perhaps: the Opportunity Party are campaigning on regulating lobbying. We often get a news article about some corruption scandal, including recently from Fonterra and Z energy, but for some reason people just seem to forget this? They forget that the government that is supposed to be representing us is essentially allowing itself to corrupt to achieve a profit, compromising our health and environment. What's alarming is that some parties are starting to realise the danger this party poses, and are trying to slander them on questionable grounds. To claim they're hypocrites because they campaign on a donation cap but don't arbitrarily force one on themselves when they still don't even hold a seat in parliament is absurd; you don't disarm your own nuclear arsenal then ask your enemy to do the same, you agree to terms *first* and *then* both begin to disarm while regularly checking on each other; something Russia and the USA did for a while. 

I find it particularly irksome that one party with an exorbitant budget was already running slander ads about three parties, and recently went back and added Opportunity as a fourth in the same image. On one level, it's telling, on another, it's lazy! With all that budget the best you could think to do was squeeze another coloured bar into an image?

As a young mathematical scientist, I believe that there is so much we could achieve from more quantitative analysis and decision making, and as a person I believe we need to end this pointless he-said-she-said and stop pushing over each others' sandcastles in the sandpit we call a parliament.
