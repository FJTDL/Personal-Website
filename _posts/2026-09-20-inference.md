I should probably admit I used to fear mathematics.

It's kind of funny, but when I was in intermediate I actually missed the day my teacher explained the classic idea of solving for an unknown number: *x*.

The result? I used to think *x* was a placeholder for 24. It's the 24th letter in the alphabet after all. But here's what is truly bizarre: during my placement exam before starting high school, my mathematics was sufficient to land in the 3rd stream—effectively meaning I was marginally better than average. In the one week before they then also streamed mathematics, I learned algebra from a very hard, cold, and strict man.

I was placed into 2nd stream mathematics. There was a hypothesis that we were actually slightly lower than the other 2nd stream class but eh, hearsay. What is funnier to me is my teachers were shocked at my dismal algebra skills and wanted to know how I had done so well in my placements. Funny answer: I did it all numerically.

See, as I struggled to understand algebra in intermediate, I took up a different approach upon arriving in high school: vary the inputs until it gets close. This eventually morphed into my way of stepping through different roots by starting with known roots either side of a number and working towards it, among other strategies.

Eventually I did in fact learn algebra; thank god. But to this day, calculus is my favourite. I especially appreciate numerical approximation techniques, as I recognise that they're the more advanced fix of what I did for years. Since learning the joy of analytical methods, I now tend to solve problems analytically while falling asleep instead of numerically, too.

The change for me came from my senior school mathematics teachers, who inflicted upon me rigorous mathematics and exceptional pedagogy. I take a great deal of solace that one of the fastest mathematicians I met in my undergrad is now training to be a teacher, as he already insists on ensuring students know arithmetic before trigonometry or algebra—atomism at its finest.

The term "mathematical maturity" apparently haunts most students on the UoA subreddit, but I agree with people needing to build this. The cynical response I used to hear from parents complaining to each other, presumably assuming that, as children, we were too stupid to understand, was that primary school teachers rarely have a good math background because "*they're all failed actors or musicians, they suck at math*". As much as I would like to push back on that... a good number of my teachers were from exactly those backgrounds, and did, in fact, suck at maths.

That's not to say all primary school teachers have poor mathematical ability—certainly I had some that were exceptional—and I think part of the problem is the "*nothing is good enough for **my** child*" mentality many parents seem to have, blaming their children's struggles in arithmetic on the teachers. If you asked a primary school teacher to find the antiderivative of a natural logarithm, then I'm certain they'd look horrified. But I think we miss the point that, well, they don't need to know it?

Don't get me wrong, I'd love a world where everyone is highly mathematically literate. I think it would be much harder to mislead people, and that many more would be in better control of their finances in such a world. But this is not reality, and the elephant in the room is: mathematics is very broad. When people picture mathematics, they often think of whiteboards covered in equations, like the sort you'd see from me when I use them. But this is based on movies of the space race, physicists, or the sort of "beautiful mind" that results in partial differential equations of maximum likelihood estimation, while leaving forgotten the worlds of graph theory, combinatorics, and a range of other such fields.

The idea of "mathematical maturity" isn't about rote learning as much mathematics as possible; it's about facing new problems from a mathematical perspective. My calculus exams from high school tried to emphasise this by combining aspects of trigonometry and numerical integration as steps to complete before solving a larger problem. Now ironically, I used the Newton-Raphson algorithm to find one of the roots in my exam after I couldn't be bothered with the algebra to find the root analytically (at the time, I still wasn't an algebra fan...). It's a great concept, but it takes time to build.

At the same time though, there is a level of "what do you really *need*?"

I present three cases:

Person A aspires to a career in statistics, but really wants to focus on fixing what he doesn't know, and what he can't easily learn by himself. So, he's taking the hardcore probability/stochastics papers while also learning about regression, operations research, and statistical computing from global experts. Not only will he develop good mathematical maturity, but he will also develop a broad mix of mathematical abilities.

Person B aspires to a research career in statistics within a particular niche. He doesn't *love* the math but he doesn't oppose it either. He will be made to take a particular paper to close a certain gap identified by a higher-up, which he doesn't oppose. He knows it will be hard, as he is skipping a pre-requisite, but he sees the value. Provided he is motivated to be there, he will do well.

Person C aspires to research in statistics education. So far she's focused on analysis programming principles, and is being "strongly" encouraged to do the same paper as person B. However, her supervisor questions the value of it, and she herself isn't particularly thrilled about the idea of the paper, especially as her research is less quantitative and more qualitative.

For these people, we have Person A who is aware of *potential* weaknesses and looking to fix them, Person B who has been made aware of a *realised* weakness and made to fix them, and Person C who is being told to fix a *far-off potential* weakness. Unlike Person A, Person C has a clear direction in statistics education, and knows that the work from that paper will not be as valuable as another in a different topic. Since Person A is yet to specialise, it makes sense for him to take this paper, and for Person B who is specialising in a field, it is necessary to. Person C may get some value from this paper down the line, but the thing we often overlook is motivation. I found those math-heavy papers to be enjoyable, but only because I really wanted to be in them. If you don't, such as Person C, then it's questionable as to if you'd even realise the value of the paper or retain any knowledge. Absolutely, Person C would get something out of it, but for a future researcher in statistics education, why would we make her fill *that* gap when we could instead have her take a course in *statistics education*? To me, that's a much more obvious gap to fill.

For myself, I was told that I would get a lot of value out of the highest level regression paper, with many claiming it would make my work in machine learning easier. My counter argument? How about I take the *machine learning paper*? I had no formal background, and while everyone claimed I had already learned more than I would get out of the course, I am currently *in* said course and finding significant value from a range of other machine learning techniques, even if that which is most relevant to my work is only six hours of content well below what I currently do. The understanding of machine learning from first principles is the value for me, but also through non-parametric models I have been able to expand my knowledge. Finally, I see *why* LDA assumes multivariate normality from a derivation; *thank god, a derivation*. 

Being formally taught neural networks from the ground up will also be valuable to me. Sure, I've been doing this for about nine months now, but I still don't really understand pooling (although most modern literature seems to avoid it too) and I'll still be grateful for an experienced mind explaining multilayer perceptrons and image processing to me, even if I'm quite comfortable with them.

This is my point though: mathematical maturity doesn't necessarily derive from the same pathways. Yes, we should all learn arithmetic and basic algebra, as well as (in my opinion) a bit of higher level algebra and calculus. From this point though? Go nuts, do what interests you. I saw today a student I have long considered quite gifted in mathematics and his understanding of statistics in a mathematics education class. Now he's a fairly awkward man, but I saw him having a great chat with his peers about the latest in mathematics education and how to explain difficult concepts to young learners. Feynman used to believe an idea was only good if you could explain it to anyone, so maybe we all ought to take a class or two in mathematics education.

As mathematicians and statisticians, we love linearity. But life is inherently non-linear, so why do we assume the same path works for everyone?
