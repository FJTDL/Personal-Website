"All models are wrong, but some are useful" - George Box, 1976

"My model looks excellent, so I know I've done something wrong" - Finn de Lange, 2026

Statistics is a funny discipline. As a student, you get used to having to check model assumptions such as a chi-squared test for goodness of fit, if your residuals are normally distributed, heteroskedacicity, normality, etc.

You learn these things to realise when they do not hold. You do this so you can correct the model, and make as good a model as possible.

Real data sucks. Real data have been dragged through the mud, spray painted blonde, set on fire, and then randomly pushed into a corner haphazardly. I'm not talking about data cleaning I mean the *genuine data itself*. If your data looks perfect, then someone has probably messed up in the collection process, or you've over-sanitised it. My work in machine learning is even worse though, because I don't really get to have proper checks like this. I have ways to check for over/underfitting absolutely, but because I do not have a parametric model, I don't have parametric assumptions to check; I have data in, prediction out.

That's not to say what I do is wrong or bad, I'd argue that it is a case-by situation. The fact industry tends to prefer non-parametric models and doesn't properly check assumptions of parametric ones is more indicative of the predictive accuracy non-parametric models have, and lower value given to explainability, as you would have with a simple linear regression, for example.

But because we always get used to things looking "a bit off" or assumptions never quite being met, such as even being taught that despite LDA being based on a multivariate normal distribution, you can get away with data that is not normally distributed, when you get a good result your first thought it usually "****"

It's because you're almost certain you messed up somewhere, and now need to go back and find where in hundreds of lines of code, or in your sampling or experiment design, you messed up.

It means you can never accept a good thing. 

But that's why my work makes me laugh. Because of the data issue, I was convinced I would obtain an attrocious fit eventually. But instead, my best predictive models are... good? Yet also... fast????

Because I have... well, bugger all samples, I find it *quite* likely my models are learning to overfit, or something like that. But even so, I find it funny that my first thought was to assume I was completely wrong. After the first good result, I needed a *week* to convince myself that what I had was actually good. Something a *lot* of students share feeling on is imposter syndrome, and I get that, I feel that way too, but to the extent I don't believe a good thing when it happens! It's a good confidence booster.
