"All models are wrong, but some are useful" - George Box, 1976

"My model looks excellent, so I know I've done something wrong" - Finn de Lange, 2026

Statistics is a funny discipline. As a student, you get used to having to check model assumptions, such as a chi-squared test for goodness of fit, if your residuals are normally distributed, heteroscedasticity, normality, etc.

You learn these things to realise when they do not hold. You do this so you can correct the model and make it as good as possible.

Real data sucks. Real data has been dragged through the mud, spray-painted blonde, set on fire, and then randomly pushed into a corner haphazardly. I'm not talking about data cleaning—I mean the *genuine data itself*. If your data looks perfect, then someone has probably messed up in the collection process, or you've over-sanitised it. My work in machine learning is even worse though, because I don't really get to have proper checks like this. I have ways to check for over/underfitting, absolutely, but because I do not have a parametric model, I don't have parametric assumptions to check; I have data in, prediction out.

That's not to say what I do is wrong or bad, I'd argue that it is a case-by-case situation. The fact industry tends to prefer non-parametric models and doesn't properly check the assumptions of parametric ones is more indicative of the predictive accuracy non-parametric models have, and the lower value given to explainability, as you would have with a simple linear regression, for example.

But because we always get used to things looking "a bit off" or assumptions never quite being met—such as even being taught that despite LDA being based on a multivariate normal distribution, you can get away with data that is not normally distributed—when you get a good result, your first thought is usually "****".

It's because you're almost certain you messed up somewhere, and now need to go back and find where in hundreds of lines of code, or in your sampling or experiment design, you made the fatal error.

It means you can never accept a good thing. 

But that's why my work makes me laugh. Because of the data issue, I was convinced I would obtain an atrocious fit eventually. But instead, my best predictive models are... good? Yet also... fast????

Because I have... well, bugger all samples, I find it *quite* likely my models are learning to overfit, or something like that. But even so, I find it funny that my first thought was to assume I was completely wrong. After the first good result, I needed a *week* to convince myself that what I had was actually good. Something a *lot* of students share feelings on is imposter syndrome, and I get that, I feel that way too—but to the extent I don't believe a good thing when it happens! It's a good confidence booster.

Personally, I always liked to confirm a result using multiple methods, but that isn't always possible. Take my work predicting if an earthquake is reported: when giving three different techniques all the same variables, we obtain 0.92 AUC with XGBoost, 0.88 with Random Forest, and 0.22 with a binomial GLM. Yes, I later found that I could improve the binomial by removing variables, but for my gluttonous approach (FEED THEM EVERYTHING) it does poorly. It means that while I'm thrilled with the performance of my tree-based ensembles, I likely need another non-parametric method as a comparison, such as a neural network, instead of my tightly restrained GLM method. It is just as well we do not use such models to make causal claims.

In my own research, I know my model always either damages the CCSN signal a bit or leaves a bit of the glitch behind—because it is wrong. But that doesn't mean it is useless.

"You can never have the true model, there never was one. Yet you may model the world as if there were one, and that is often enough." - Finn again, still 2026
