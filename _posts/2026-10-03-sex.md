Yeah nah, I'm talking about sex ed in particular. Why is my title misleading? Because people still get too squeamish about sex, but I don't think those people understand how dangerous that squeamishness actually is.

In the past week, I had four major concerning moments:

1. Realising the way condom failure rates are advertised is based on assumptions they do not state.
2. Meeting 4 people, a mix of men and women, who did not know the difference between the vagina and the vulva.
3. Learning a friend of mine (female) did not know that the penis has two states (flaccid and erect) and believed men always existed in the flaccid state.
4. Realising many people believe "one size fits all" for condoms.

It made me cast my mind back to my schooling on sex ed within the education system. But first, I should explain that one benefit to growing up in a family of scientists is you will always get the truth. It means that from a young age I had no misconceptions about "where I came from," as many children wonder at some point. It also meant that my school-mandated sex ed classes were something I could always consider from a detached perspective; there was nothing they taught that I didn't already know, after all. And why is that? Because they never taught anything useful. First, a history.

My primary school, beyond generally being a fairly unpleasant place, handled puberty education as a once-a-year deal for Year 3 or 4 (memory fails me) through Year 6. This involved dividing girls and boys into two classes and teaching them about "the changes that will happen to your body," generally promoting things like using deodorant and changing underpants daily. We've all had to tolerate unhygienic people, and objectively, for the age group, this is actually the right thing to do. At this age, children are still, well, children. Exposing them to the full organ system all at once is going to be a lot to do in two hours, much less in a co-ed situation, so I give this a pass.

Intermediate was much better. Beyond my opinion that my intermediate was, on the whole, a fantastic school, I also believe they handled health education very well. Although I do remember studying the Tokyo sarin gas attack, the 1936 Olympics, and the Israel-Palestine conflict (which stretches the idea of "health" a bit far), the system for sex ed was more detailed than primary. Most importantly, they taught by form class, which was co-ed. This means the girls learn about the boys and the boys learn about the girls, all while learning about themselves. Yes, sometimes someone misbehaved, and I remember my class being horrified by how bored I was while they all felt "weird." Truth be told, the anatomy book I'd been reading in my spare time had lifted the curtain on the majority of what was taught. On the whole, I think this gets a pass as well.

High school **fails**.

Unlike a primary school that teaches about changes to your body, and an intermediate where they explain periods and basic anatomy, the role of high school sex ed *should* be about sex, right? But the problem from the get-go is that it was done by form class, which was segregated. "Hello fellow men, what say we ignore the female reproductive system for 20 weeks and instead learn not to mix vodka and Red Bull? Did you know meth is bad for your health? You have a penis! Here's how to check for testicular cancer in a shower! By the way, we're only going to give you one year of health/sex ed classes while you're 13, the rest is up to you."

The fact that we stop teaching sex ed as much as three *years* before people can legally provide consent means people will forget *a lot* of this before they ever reach that level of maturity. We often hear discussions about consent and what consent is, and what it should be. More recently, political discussion has considered if there needs to be a legal definition for consent. It's a very important topic, and it definitely should be educated. But, by stopping at such a young age, we end up having a plausible deniability for why it isn't taught: "it wasn't relevant at the time."

As for the content, I wish I were joking. The genuine extent of the actual sex ed, beyond a bit more male anatomy, was learning about menstrual products because "your girlfriend will thank you." Otherwise, I can tell you all about why not to mix Red Bull and vodka.

Why this is particularly bizarre to me is that it's no secret the female anatomy is more complex than the male. A crude analogy: men are a soap dispenser running low that needs a few pumps; women have a complicated web of hormones, organs, and so much more. I took a medical sciences paper at university where we spent a week or two on female anatomy. When asked if we would cover male anatomy, the lecturer looked up, confused, and said, "Well we could, but there just isn't much to cover..." To this day, I am grateful that younger Finn wanted to be a doctor. It helps you understand your own body, sure, but also those around you—especially a partner. Those few weeks of female anatomy helped immensely, as did the rest of "Environmental Threats to Human Health," a great albeit time-consuming paper.

To me, being told that understanding sanitary products for an event that happens once a month is "enough" to know about the female body as a male is blatantly wrong. You don't need an intricate understanding of luteinising hormone—God knows mine is insufficient—but you *should* understand the anatomy of your partner, and perhaps more importantly, how actual sexual intercourse works.

...oh yeah, **sex**.

It seems that throughout my entire schooling, sex ed never actually mentioned sex itself. I have spent a day actively trying to recall if we ever discussed it, but I can't! What's worse, **no one** I have spoken to can remember if it was taught either! Most of us remember putting a condom on a banana or a wooden penis (must be weird working in that factory), but no one remembers learning about sex. 

Condoms usually report a 2% fail rate. That 2% fail rate assumes you use them perfectly. Often, clinical trials find the true fail rate (due to improper use) is closer to 13%-15%. These numbers are also calculated assuming a certain level of use, which will obviously vary depending on how active you are. But in this modern information age, when you can't work out the math, what do you do? You ask AI. I recently prompted an AI on this problem and, as a statistician, the information it gave me as gospel was so wrong it was borderline dangerous. My favourite falsehood was the claim that if a condom fails in a year, no more will fail for the rest of the year. 

I prompted it some more, and this hallucination was based on the assumption that a pregnancy is carried to term, and that it therefore "clocks into next year, resetting the risk." This completely negates the fact that a pregnancy may not be carried to term, or that if you got pregnant in January and deliver in October, there are still two months of the year left for a condom to fail (the fact that I'm even arguing this second point is completely absurd).

But it really begs the question of where people go to learn. There are some who claim to learn from porn, but there are profound dangers in how porn affects a young mind and warps perceptions of relationships. Funnily enough, there are a great many online sites you probably didn't know existed dedicated to educating on sexual health, which are extremely beneficial.

Naturally, you begin to wonder how long this generational misunderstanding has been going on. The saddest instance I know of is a friend of mine whose sex life was painful. When she asked her mother for advice, her mother's response was, "Just do nothing, be as immobile as possible, it hurts less." It goes without saying this should *never* be the case. Sex should be enjoyable for both parties, and they should be able to communicate freely about how they feel physically and emotionally. Yet here is a role model, a parent, informing her child that the solution was to give up and tolerate it—meaning it's something she only *endured* instead of *enjoyed*—and she perpetuates that mentality a generation further by teaching her daughter this. 

It goes both ways. I was speaking to an older man who raised sons my age, asking his opinion on the matter. He responded that when he was 18 and asked his father where the vagina was to actually have sex, his father had grumpily told him, "You know where to stick it." Basic female anatomy dictates that when you "just stick it," you're probably in the wrong place and probably just hurting her. He told me that he eventually asked his doctor for advice and got a much better answer.
