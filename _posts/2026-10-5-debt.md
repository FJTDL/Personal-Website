Debt is *massively* misunderstood.

Lately, I have been meeting more and more people who misunderstand finance. They firmly believe debt is some evil wizard ready to enchant them with capital, only to curse them with a high interest rate.

To be fair, they aren't completely wrong.

The problem with debt is how it is perceived. In my experience, most people perceive a prison. Perhaps chains.

In reality, debt is a double-edged sword. It provides the ability to do things you previously could not do, with the caveat that you must be able to repay your obligations in a timely manner. A great many people work in organisations that borrow (take debt) yet still misunderstand it. However, I think this is a blameless ignorance. When were you ever taught about debt in school? Commonly discussed in my high school years was the idea of a "life skills" class. Cooking, driving, money management, government structure, and a host of other topics were lamented by my peers as vague or totally alien. For me, it's taken years of additional learning to understand these only enough to survive; only this year did I learn of the mechanism for an overhang government in NZ, despite three years of having 123 seats instead of 120. (Indeed, I also believe that sex ed is failing, but that was a topic for another post.)

A "life skills" course would be strongly beneficial to anyone leaving school. The reason I think debt is so important to discuss is because a great many students will go on to join universities or polytechs where they will need a student loan. Because of online discourse, the idea of how these loans work is often warped by the systems of other countries. On a slightly different note, I was in the sauna at the gym listening to a group of women arguing about how important a credit card is for building your credit score. One, an American, was quite aggressively informing the others that they'd never buy a house if they didn't get a credit card. While this mostly true for the US (it is very difficult without a credit card there), it isn't completely true, and especially not the case in NZ. It speaks to the broader misunderstanding of the financial system and what debt and credit truly are.

Student debt in NZ is quite interesting. What is commonly misunderstood is that you don't accrue interest unless you leave the country for more than six months. This means the loan is essentially shrinking in real value every year due to inflation. Because of this, the optimal mathematical strategy is to pay only the absolute minimum required by the IRD, and put any extra cash you have into safe assets or investments. Even with a basic term deposit, you profit off the interest earned. Yet, in my experience, many people who fear owing money will rush to pay off their student loan as fast as possible. This is actually somewhat ironic; by aggressively paying down a 0% interest loan instead of investing that money, they are mathematically making themselves poorer just to buy peace of mind.

A key driver in the fear of debt seems to be movies. Most debt-fearers I speak with mention some far-flung situation where the bank is actively trying to repossess a house to sell it for a profit, or how debt collectors will hurt you for late payments. Movies warping perceptions of reality—such as the claim violent movies increase violence (not that I think a causal link has been made)—are commonplace nowadays, so it's not surprising to see the same warping applied to the financial system. Naturally, these extreme situations are not the case, provided we look at who we generally acquire debt from.

Consider a bank, and your desire to buy a house. You approach a bank for a mortgage. The bank spends a lot of time auditing your financial behaviour, assessing your income, checking credit scores, and eventually approves you for a mortgage with an interest rate. Right away, notice that you approached the bank because you *couldn't* afford the house outright—you needed help. And, in order to make this fair to the bank, they charge you interest. Off the bat, let's talk about that interest.

Interest is the cost of debt. When you borrow money, it doesn't come for free. You are charged interest because you are using money that does not *belong* to you, so you must pay for having used it. This interest is what gets paid into a bank account. The difference between the interest rate on your savings account and the mortgage rates for the same bank is what the bank takes as its cut. In this way, consider the interest rate to be the money in a sale—the thing you give up to make the deal fair.

So, naturally, you want the lowest interest rate, as that is the cheapest deal. In exchange, you may think the bank wants the highest interest rate. They do, but within *reason*. Supply and demand controls interest rates too: if the rates are too high, no one borrows, and if too low, everyone borrows, so we need a happy medium. Similarly, the bank *definitely* doesn't want you to default. That's right—the bank doesn't want your home. Why? Because the bank isn't financing you with their money either; they're financing you with mine. The bank takes money from the savings accounts of everyday people and businesses, and invests that in you to buy your house. Because of that, they have an obligation to me and others like me to at least return our money, but also to pay us back for using it (our interest rate). If you default, the bank has to go through the lengthy and often expensive process of repossessing your house, selling it, and redistributing the money back to the creditors/debt-holders (those of us who financed your debt). Because this involves a cost to the bank through needing realtors and time, they really don't want to do this, and if too much of this happens, we get financial crises. Part of the reason a savings account may have delays for moving your money around—and why term deposits where you can't access your money exist—is to give banks the confidence to use that money for longer-term investments that yield higher returns.

Now we reach bonds. Bonds are the ultimate happy medium between your safe but slow savings account and volatile but potentially lucrative stocks. Even a savings account bears risk, as there is still the potential for a whole bunch of things to go wrong with a bank, and you could still lose money (think the 2008 GFC). This is still quite a low risk, and it's also why it takes so long to get approved for borrowing; government rules enforce banks to usually only lend to those who meet a certain bar in order to protect the economy, and those who save with the bank. Meanwhile, shares offer the ultimate potential for unlimited return and total bankruptcy. There is *no* guarantee what a stock does, and even if it pays a dividend, it may still depreciate more than the value of the dividend. For this reason, we now have a continuum of low risk/low return and high risk/high potential. Bonds sit right in the middle. 

A bond is just like a mortgage, but for organisations like governments and businesses to do things they otherwise couldn't afford. However, as our interest rule from earlier states, they need to pay us a token: an interest rate. This is paid to you regularly over the period of the bond, much like your interest in your savings account, and at maturity, you are reimbursed the full principal. In essence, you get your money back in full, plus interest. If the organisation goes bankrupt, then when placed into administration you, as a debt holder, still have priority. Where possible, you will get money back. The shareholder doesn't; they just lose everything.

So bonds seem great? Well, if you're a patient investor, they are great—especially as a means to balance out a riskier portfolio with some guarantee. They certainly do have some risks, and they're beholden to inflation in terms of their value. If you need to liquidate them early, there is a decent risk of having lost money (although equally, of having gained money). 

However, what's clear with this is the misunderstanding of debt. With the democratisation of the share market through apps allowing ownership of fractional shares to the average person, many now see the potential for investing in shares. Debt, however, makes the world go round. Buying shares does relatively little for the productivity of the business beyond perhaps borrowing against its own valuation, but through debt, we allow these businesses to grow and improve the economic situation.

Democratisation of the debt markets would allow for greater availability of debt, supporting the growth of businesses and thus the economy, while also enabling access to higher potential returns than a traditional savings account.

Still afraid of debt? Try owning some.
