"Polls are useless" no, polls are funny.

A good quote I heard once was "reweighting is really the only reason polls aren't completely useless", and I am inclined to agree, except at the end of the day we have a very difficult time justifying quite how our weightings are applied for a population we have uncertainty about. More importantly, many polls are *biased*, but that's why some polls are more useful than others, and why they depend on their purpose.

Objectively, you could use a misleading poll as propaganda, either to make opponents give up, or convince swing voters to join you. But polls are designed to be informative about a population, and that's why the poll-of-polls is generally more reliable. It's quite similar conceptually to the random forest algorithm - overfit as many trees as you like (knowing more is better), and average over them to get a better perception of reality. This isn't perfect, it's still very possible that every poll you poll is biased in the same direction, or that there is some other form of imbalance you don't know about (or equally *they* don't know about!).

We can devise a great many ways to rebalance for this, but it's never going to be perfect, and that's ok. Statistics is all about drawing conclusions of random variables knowing that there will be some level of irreproducible noise, and although humans and especially human opinions are a messy one to understand, it's still somewhat possible. It's much akin to the jelly beans in a jar problem. If you have an unknown amount of jelly beans in a jar, and have an audience call out their estimates and you average that, the averages usually aren't especially bad estimates! It's called "wisdom of the crowds" which essentially goes back to the same idea of random forest: Smooth over lots of bias to obtain the truth.

We can do similar with polls, and yes there will always be some level of noise we don't understand; if there wasn't, then we'd be called deterministicians! Below are some plots I made in R for this very musing. You can see some consistent trends, such as the sudden explosion of the Opportunities party in recent times, and the gradual drop-off for Te Pati in recent times, but you can very clearly see that the trends are inconsistent. Looking at the National party, while all organisations show some form of drop off with time, the Taxpayers Union-Curia shows a very different behaviour in recent times - this is known to be a somewhat biased poll, however. But this is good, as we have obtained variance in our trends. We know this election is more hotly contested than any in recent memory, so we expect some variance, bias, noise, and drift!

What's more interesting is to look at the weighted averages of these polls by party. Weighted here meaning by the sample size of each poll, with larger samples being given more weight; this is still biased! I do not know which polls lean which way or if the samples obtained were fair, and I assume a level of reweighting from the poll producers that is absolutely wrong, purely because it can never be right! But what we see in this averaged bias is distinctive behaviours:

1. All bar one poll show Labour rising to a peak in January 2026 then back down to a more baseline more inline with National.

2. National is decreasing in all polls, usually at similar rates.

3. Te Pati have been wounded by their internal party dilemma earlier in the year, with all bar one poll showing this. The same bar one is also that showing a different relationship for Labour, which also seems to be highly variant across all party plots, and a quick Google search indicates that this indeed may be a biased poll, which might explain why it has parties such as Green and Te Pati with much lower estimates than other polls.

4. Opportunities dipped then saw a meteoric spike across *all* polls.

<img src="./Static/weighted_average.png" alt="weighted averages" style="max-width: 100%; height: auto;" />

There are a few other key trends we can observe in the likes of NSF looking something like a logistic curve and ACT looking like disorganised spaghetti, both being extremely curious behaviours, but equally, when looking at multi-coloured lines, we can read into things too deeply when there is really nothing there.

But what my weighted-average black line shows appears to be a fair estimate for any of these parties, and appears to nicely smooth over the more unusual times quite well, demonstrating that the wisdom of the crowds allows us to ignore particularly odd ideas.
