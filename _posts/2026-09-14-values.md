Aristotle believed that there were 11 virtues, which in a mathematical sense, are best described by negative quadratics. What I mean by this is that these virtues are reflected by being a "middle" of two extremes of this virtue. For example, pride. Too little pride and you lack confidence, or perhaps you present yourself poorly, etc. Too much pride and you lean into overconfidence and arrogance. So, the happy medium is the optimal place to have the virtue of pride.

Virtues and values are often overlooked in modern society or I daresay "weaponised". Discourse on ethical matters or what is a right can be considered an argument of peresonal values, but the problem is that although we still call these things debate, the opposing sides have no interest in the other party's argument and only want to make the other feel stupid and insignificant. I have my own opinions on these matters, but when I find myself faced with someone strongly in an opposing camp, I feel no desire to argue (frankly it's pointless) but I do want to understand *why*. It's not that I seek to pull apart their argument, but I want to understand their thought process. One thing I try to emphasise to people is that I do not judge anyone's decisions provided they have genuinely thought about it. Although a decision may seem weird to us, there are certain intrinsic aspects that person cannot put into words for their decision. Even if they backtrack on their decision later, it doesn't mean it was the wrong decision at the time, it was simply that in their own mind, with what was known at the time, they made a concious decision they find best. It's why voting is a curious problem.

Socrates was suspicious of democracy, providing the analogy that when choosing a captain of the boat you are about to sail on, you choose the best captain available, not the one by popular opinion (although a solid argument can be made for wisom of the crowds, but ignore my diatribe). The oldest form of democracy, in Athens, had stipulations on sex, education, and freedom in order to be allowed to vote. The idea here was that these men would be able to make informed decisions in the best interest of the populace - although ignoring the voices of women, workers, and slaves seems like an interesting way to represent a "populace". Today, voting is generally a right, not a privelege that is earned, and there are arguments for both sides as to which is right or wrong. Seeing as politics is a human invention not governed by any physical laws, I don't see how it's possible to even *have* right and wrong, but that is besides the point.

Returning to Aristotle, when hearing of the concept of virtues a great many statisticians and social scientists think "*aha! I can use factor analysis here*!" which you could, but that is only a proxy variable, and what variables do you use to model the factor anyway? How do you measure them? I think at some point in our lives, we all hit low points or dark patches. What I find interesting was a theory a counselor I knew once mentioned was the idea of value violation. The idea here is that everyone has a set of core values, such as happiness, freedom, financial security and the like, which when these values are not met, we become unhappy. He sent me a link to try, and while I believe that some of these values (such as *health*) would be valued by everyone, I find it very interesting that the people I have convinced to do this I find very easy to get along with, because I know how to meet their values. For example, I know someone for whom certainty is her ultimate value. When we agree to meet at 0800 hours, I need to be there *by 0800 hours* or she will quickly feel like her needs aren't being met. Really, that's a fair argument; we all hate someone who is late! For me, I find the lack of curiousity alarming. Granted, I am not particularly driven to explore the world in that sense of curiousity, but if you tell me something works I want to know *why*.

Religion is a common battleground, and I refuse to get invovled, but what I find most interesting is putting aside the afterlife, deities, and how it informs your voting, religions propagate values. Considering Christianity, the idea to "love your neighbour" is really about treating those around you with good grace. This makes sense as it makes life more pleasant for everyone if we all get along, but why your neighbour? Because you see them. You live next to them. A neighbour is someone you have good potential to have friction with, so a good relationship reduces this. Islam is the youngest abrahamic religion, and it is well known muslims do not eat pork. Pork is perceived as "unclean". It is believed that this stems from the fact pork is banned in Judaism due to how unclean the meat was in ancient times (parasites), and that early Islam may have adopted this as they recognised the harm this meat was causing based on how it was handled in other religions. This is where I think religion is a good thing, as it can be used to extend messages of value and health. Yes, it also has the potential to spread misinformation and harm, but I am not seeking to debate the value of religion, only the way it can be used to spread messages of virtuosity. 

What I find interesting about religion is how people choose to approach the afterlife. The oldest Hebrew and Greek texts of the bible make no explicit mention to hell. Hell has become a punishment for those who according to Chrisitianty, are living without virtue and behaving in ways that are not consistent with the faith. This has led to some denominations being obsessed with the fear of going to hell, and some people who spend their entire lives trying to "save souls" from heading there, and being obsessed with joining heaven after their death. What is interesting here, is it seems that the oldest versions of Christianity were more around "those who live with best virtue are rewarded, and the rest are not" while there are now interpretations of "those who live with best virtue are rewarded, and the rest are punished". Both interpretations are fine; like I said with politics, interpretation is up to you. I am an atheist. Not agnostic, and atheist. However, I would like to hope that if a religion is true, that it is one where the prime mover is benevolent towards our species and the mistakes of the individual. Although not religious, I believe in redemption the same way three eagles went to Mordor that day.

I do *not* mean to offend anyone. I find religion quite a beautiful thing, even if I believe there are many who interpret it in ways that harm others, but when you look at the beauty of Westminster Abbey, Notre-Dame, the Sagrada Familia, and Mecca, you must acknowledge that we produce some amazing things as a species. I find Mecca a particular sad one for me, as I would genuinely love to visit Mecca. However, as an non-muslim, I cannot. I find this sad, but I acknowledge that this is not *my place*. It is a holy place for those who follow that religion. Although not a Christian, I find the churches I listed before to also be beautiful. A very fond memory for me as a child was lighting candles in these places following the death of a family member. Although not for religious reasons, there is a level of reverence for the place I was and the significance of the gesture in that faith.

I find the classic argument of Onchocerciasis challenging the existence of god, as why would he make such a thing, to be overused. I also find the counter argument that it was the challenge of that child or their family, to be weak. For myself, I look to the creation of the universe with a level of skepticism on the whole. Although Hawking believed that the universe could spontaneously exist out of nothing through the laws of physics, we cannot definitively prove anything. I find the idea of there being nothing, then something, a bit absurd, and this somewhat informs my absurdist view on the universe. If there is a prime mover before the universe, I wonder if perhaps this being was and perhaps still is, conducting an experiment. Many people of faith are accepting some scientific ideas such as evolution, so just bear with me: what is the universe was created by a being but only in it's most primitive form? What it was simply BANG, here's a lot of energy? We know that mass and energy have an equivalence so that is handled, the only other thing we need is time, as gravity is simply a warping of spacetime due to mass. With that established, what if *everything* else is simply coincidence? That includes life, by the way.

What I find amusing about this theory is that it means that there is no meaning to life. While some schools of thought dictate the same, but that you can create your own meaning, I object on the basis of meaning being a personal value. Bringing us full circle to values, my motivation in my life is curiousity. I enjoy tackling problems to see what I can learn, I am a scientist in many ways. Yes my work has spanned educations, politics, astrophysics, botany, and a range of other problems, and I would probably enjoy many of the prediction problems a bank has such as fraud and defaulting in the same way I enjoy predicting the properties of stars dying. I find stars dying *more* interesting, as there is so much we do not know, but I fundamentally enjoy the practice of statistcs in a range fields; just particularly in science, and physics more precisely. Seeing the parallels to other fields is fascinating, and led to my recent work in polling. However, I still enjoy my gravitational waves the most as I find it most interesting. Does this give me meaning? Not personally, I don't really define "meaning" to my person, but I believe in importance. Importance is also defined subjectively, and while I believe my work for extending human knowledge of our universe is important, I find the works of teachers and doctors extremely important. Why? I value intelligence and curiousity, so my own work hits that, but we need good education to achieve this, and my value in health and certainty is reflected by my reverence for the medical professional: I can't do anything interesting if I'm dead.
